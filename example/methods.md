## About this document

This document gives every result of the variables extracted from the vibration signals, their correlations with the subcutaneous fat thickness and the muscle activation check, with each participant's values, from [@trama2021|n]. The methods are the article's. The statistics differ slightly: each participant's mean values were compared with a permutation analysis of variance (see Statistics), where the article fitted linear mixed models to every trial, and the correlations include the peak acceleration. The power-map analysis (statistical parametric mapping) is reported in the article only.

## Participants

A group of {n} participants composed of {count:sex=M} men and {count:sex=F} women ({mean_sd:age} years, {mean_sd:height} cm, {mean_sd:mass} kg) volunteered to participate in this study. All the participants were healthy and free from lower-limb injury. The study was approved by the local university ethics committee, according to the statement of ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the participants signed an informed consent document.

## Instrumentation

An electromyogram (EMG) of the vastus lateralis of the right leg was recorded at 1000 Hz with pairs of surface electrodes (Delsys, Natick, Massachusetts, USA). An Aixplorer ultrasound device was used with an SL15-4 probe (Supersonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) to obtain video of the vastus lateralis vibrations (1000 Hz). The soft tissue acceleration of the thigh as well as the probe acceleration (±50 g, 1000 Hz) were recorded with two biaxial accelerometers (Mega Electronics, Kuopio, Finland).

## Procedure and data collection

Participants were seated on a custom-built isometric knee-extensor ergometer, with the hip and knee angles set at 90° and the chest secured with a safety belt. After having shaved and cleaned the skin with alcohol, surface electromyographic electrodes were placed on the vastus lateralis belly, according to SENIAM recommendations. Before the experiment, participants performed a standardized warm-up consisting of ten incremental isometric knee extensions of 5 seconds. After a 3-minute rest period, participants performed two maximum voluntary isometric contractions to determine the maximal EMG signal and normalize the vastus lateralis activation level.

Testing consisted of an assessment of vastus lateralis accelerations induced by an external shock, using a pendulum impactor (a reflex hammer dropped from an angle of 45° with respect to the vertical). The position of the impactor was adjusted so that the impact was administered 6 cm distally from the location of the muscle-vibration measurement device. Shocks were administered under a relaxed condition (4 shocks) and a contracted condition (4 shocks at 40% of the maximal activation). Visual feedback of the 0.05-second normalized root-mean-square EMG of the vastus lateralis was displayed on a computer screen to help the participant achieve the 40% ± 10% target activation level. The impact was administered when the activation level was reached.

For each condition, two-dimensional vastus lateralis vibrations were measured with both an ultrasound device and an accelerometer. The ultrasound probe was positioned perpendicularly to the longitudinal axis of the thigh and proximally to the EMG electrodes, so that the muscle vibrations were analysed along the mediolateral and anteroposterior axes of the thigh. The participant being seated, this document calls them the **lateral** (mediolateral) and **vertical** (anteroposterior) axes. The position of the probe was marked on the skin, and the probe was clamped with a mechanical arm. A water-based gel was applied between the skin and the probe to avoid probe-skin contact. The position of the probe was adjusted between the relaxed and contracted conditions to keep the probe out of contact with the skin when the muscle expanded, and therefore avoid any pressure from the probe on the skin. Once all the shocks were administered, the probe was replaced by the accelerometer, which was placed at the same location and oriented in the same direction as the probe. Thereafter, the same procedure was performed. Finally, the subcutaneous thigh fat thickness was measured with a skin caliper (the average of three measurements) at the location of the ultrasound probe and the accelerometer ({mean_sd:fat_thickness} mm, from {range:fat_thickness} mm).

## Data treatment

The data were processed with MATLAB R2018b (MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA). Raw EMG signals of the vastus lateralis were run through a Butterworth band-pass filter between 10 Hz and 499 Hz. Thereafter, the root-mean-square envelope of the signal was calculated with a window length of 0.05 seconds. The signals were normalized for each participant with the mean value of the greatest 0.5-second plateau obtained during the two maximal contractions. The mean EMG was calculated from 0.5 seconds to 0.05 seconds before the impact, to avoid the noise caused by the shock.

Vastus lateralis accelerations were first computed from the ultrasonographic images (bandwidth 7.5 MHz, acquisition frequency 1000 Hz, resolution 256 × 180 pixels, field of view 5 × 2.5 cm). The upper zone of the muscle, just below the subcutaneous fat, was manually selected, and the binary robust invariant scalable keypoints (BRISK) algorithm detected a maximum of 300 points within it. The point trajectories were tracked over time to obtain the mediolateral and anteroposterior displacements of each point. A Butterworth band-pass filter between 10 Hz and 130 Hz was applied to the displacements, which were then derived twice to obtain the point accelerations in the two directions. Points with a maximum acceleration above 5 m·s⁻² were included, and the mean acceleration of these points was analysed. The acceleration signals of the accelerometers (skin and probe) were filtered with a Butterworth band-pass filter between 10 Hz and 130 Hz.

The acceleration signals were investigated in the time-frequency domain with a continuous wavelet transform, using a Morse mother wavelet with γ = 3 and P² = 60. The modulus of the coefficients was expressed as power as a function of time and frequency, *p*(*t*, *f*), between 10 Hz and 130 Hz during the 0.25 seconds after the impact. The **main frequency** was the frequency with the most energy in the energy spectrum *E*(*f*), the integral of the power over time, and the **median frequency** the frequency that split that spectrum into two equal parts. The **damping** coefficient *D* was obtained by fitting the theoretical power decay *P*<sub>T1</sub>·e<sup>−*D*·*t*</sup> to the overall power *P*(*t*), the integral of the power over frequency, from the instant of maximal power decay (T1) to the instant when the power reached 10% of its value at T1. The **peak acceleration** was the largest acceleration of the filtered signal after the impact.

Trials for which the peak acceleration of the ultrasound probe was above 5 m·s⁻² on at least one axis were excluded. Each variable is each participant's mean over their retained trials (up to four per condition).

## Statistics

Each variable was compared, separately on each axis, with a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance: device (accelerometry, ultrasonography) × activation level (relaxed, contracted). The mean EMG does not depend on the axis and was analysed once. The p-values came from permutations: the residuals of the model without the tested effect were sign-flipped participant by participant ([@freedman1983]; [@winkler2014]). With {n} participants there are {n_perm} distinct sign patterns, and every one of them was used, so each p-value is exact; the smallest possible is {p_min}.

When the interaction was significant, the two devices were compared within each activation level, and the two activation levels within each device, with paired t statistics on the same sign flips. These four comparisons were corrected together (single-step minimum p). Without a significant interaction, the same comparisons are shown pale: they are outside the interaction and mostly reflect the main effects. Effect sizes are Hedges' g. A result was significant when p ≤ {alpha}.

For each vibration variable, axis and activation level, the relative difference between the devices, 100 × (accelerometry − ultrasonography) / ultrasonography, was computed for each participant and correlated with the subcutaneous fat thickness (Pearson's r, with the least-squares slope). The {n_corr} p-values of each axis (t test of r) were corrected together with the Holm–Bonferroni method, the axes being analysed separately as in the analyses of variance. A significant correlation was called strong when |r| was between 0.6 and 0.79, and very strong when |r| ≥ 0.8.

The article analysed the same variables with linear mixed models on every trial (likelihood-ratio tests, Holm–Bonferroni correction, Tukey post hoc tests), and tested 12 correlations (the peak acceleration aside), whose figure gives uncorrected p-values. Its p-values and effect sizes can therefore differ slightly from these.
